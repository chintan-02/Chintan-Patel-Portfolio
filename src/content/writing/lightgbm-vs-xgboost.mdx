## The setup

The source dataset contains 366,890 emergency-department records and 972 columns. After scoping the deployment model to ESI 3, ESI 4, and ESI 5, the final modeling subset contained 257,306 records and the runtime feature builder produced 273 model features.

For structured tabular data, gradient-boosted trees were the natural starting point. The real question was not simply “LightGBM or XGBoost?” It was “which candidate gives the best balance of overall performance, class-level behaviour, and safety-sensitive downgrade risk?”

The comparison included LightGBM, XGBoost, Random Forest, a Dense Neural Network, an RNN, and a GRU. The sequence models were useful challengers, but the structured tabular problem did not gain enough from sequence architectures to justify their additional complexity.

## Why accuracy was not the deciding metric

The final model predicts three classes, but those classes are not equally important.

A model can improve overall accuracy while making more unsafe ESI 3-to-ESI 5 downgrades. That would be a worse system even if the headline number looked better.

I therefore compared candidates using:

- macro F1
- weighted F1
- ESI 5 F1
- class-level confusion patterns
- unsafe ESI 3-to-ESI 5 downgrade rate
- deployment complexity and reproducibility

## What separated the candidates

LightGBM and XGBoost were close enough on raw accuracy that choosing only from that number would have been weak engineering.

LightGBM provided the stronger final combination of:

- weighted and macro F1
- ESI 5 behaviour
- lower unsafe downgrade risk
- efficient training and inference for high-dimensional tabular features
- a straightforward text-model artifact for the FastAPI registry

Calibration was evaluated, but the raw LightGBM probabilities were retained because the calibrated challengers did not preserve all configured guardrails.

## Why the model does not predict ESI 1 or ESI 2

The deployment model is intentionally scoped to the classes for which the final evaluation evidence is strongest.

Higher-acuity cases are handled through transparent safety rules and clinician review. This keeps the machine-learning claim precise and makes safety escalation visible instead of burying it inside a poorly supported rare-class prediction.

## What I would do next

The next model-quality work would focus on:

- additional external or temporally separated validation
- calibration and confidence interpretation under changing data
- subgroup and error analysis
- model-drift monitoring
- challenger experiments only when they improve measured safety or maintainability

The general lesson is not that LightGBM always beats XGBoost. The lesson is that the evaluation metric must reflect the real cost of being wrong, and the final architecture must be honest about what the model is and is not qualified to predict.
